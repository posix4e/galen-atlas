Much of Galen remains inaccessible in English.

Galen of Pergamon (129 – c. 216 CE) is the largest surviving author of Greco-Roman antiquity, with millions of words preserved and a corpus commonly estimated at about a tenth of surviving Greek literature from before 350 CE. Much of it remains available only in ancient Greek, medieval Arabic, or Latin editions.

Method

Reading the Greek against its witnesses

A great deal of Galen survives in more than one line of transmission: the Greek direct tradition, Arabic translations made from now-lost Greek exemplars, and old Latin versions. The versions are indirect tradition — evidence about the text rather than copies of it — but their exemplars may antedate the surviving Greek, so a versional reading can come from an independent branch of the stemma. Of any divergence we ask which underlying Greek would account for it, and whether what we have is a variant in the text or a construal of it. The Arabic witnesses we hold are listed with their provenance.

At Aphorisms i.1 the Greek καιρὸς ὀξύς hangs between two senses: the critical moment is acute, or the time is short. Ḥunayn renders it الوقت ضيّق, “the time is narrow.” We first published that as a second witness converging on the temporal reading. It is nothing of the kind: Ḥunayn was translating Galen's commentary, in which the gloss ὀλιγοχρόνιον stands a few lines below the lemma, so his rendering follows from the exposition he had in hand. What this site is

Three things, all in the open:

  1. An atlas. A tracking table of the surviving corpus and an interactive text map: each work, the language it survives in, where the text is digitized, whether an English translation exists, and the documented paths into Arabic, Hebrew, Latin, and modern editions. Each entry is flagged by how well its status has been verified, because half the battle is simply knowing what's missing. Coverage today: the 97 works digitized by First1KGreek plus 11 added by hand; roughly two dozen more surviving works are still to be entered.
  2. A roadmap. A plan for translating the untranslated, starting with texts whose Greek is already digitized, and a public vote on which work gets the first full campaign.
  3. Working translations. A verse of the Commentary on the Aphorisms, the Greek collated with Ḥunayn's Arabic; and the opening of the compound-drug book on the scalp — hitherto without a published English rendering — as an unreviewed project draft.

Honesty first. This is an amateur, AI-assisted project, not a critical edition. Drafts are published with their source, citations, uncertainty, and translation rationale so readers can catch mistakes. The catalogue is preliminary; “unverified” and “needs research” mean exactly that.

Historical text only. Ancient remedies may be toxic or unsafe. Pergamap is not medical advice; do not prepare or use remedies described in these texts.

How to help

  • Vote on the ballot for the work that gets the first full campaign.
  • Verify an entry. Pick any work marked “unverified” in the corpus table, find out whether an English translation exists, and file an issue or pull request with your source.
  • Translate a chunk. If you read Greek (or Arabic, or want to review AI drafts), see CONTRIBUTING.

How to contribute via pull request

The whole site is a public repository of plain HTML, JavaScript, and versioned JSON — no framework or production build step.

  1. Fork posix4e/pergamap on GitHub and make a branch.
  2. Fixing the catalogue? Edit data/works.json — update english.status, add a structured citation, and set english.verification.status to checked only with a dated source reference.
  3. Adding a translation? Use the harness to generate a manageable, source-pinned packet. Fill in the English, citations, and a concise translation rationale. First claim the range in an issue.
  4. Preview locally with python3 -m http.server 8000; opening files through file:// cannot load the JSON datasets.
  5. Open the pull request — one work or one chunk per PR, sources cited. Review happens in the open, arguing from the Greek. Merged means live.
