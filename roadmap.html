Roadmap

The goal is simple to say and long to do: get the untranslated Galen into readable English, in public, with the source text alongside every claim.

How we pick targets

  • No existing English. We don't duplicate the Loeb, CMG, or other published translations — the atlas exists partly so effort goes where nothing exists.
  • A digitized source text. Machine-readable Greek (or a published Arabic edition) so work can start immediately, with the original published next to the translation.
  • Bounded and self-contained. A book or treatise that can actually be finished, not an open-ended slog.
  • Human interest. Texts that show ancient life — recipes, patients, prices, complaints — over technical scaffolding, at least at first.

Phase 0 — Map the corpus live now

Publish the atlas, with translation status flagged by confidence, and open it to corrections. The unglamorous foundation: you can't plan a rescue without a map.

Phase 1 — Pilot: the hair & scalp book begun

Book 1 of On the Composition of Drugs According to Places (Kühn XII 378–496): Galen's remedies for baldness, falling hair, hair dye, depilatories, dandruff, lice, and scalp sores — ten chapters, self-contained, vivid, and never in English. The preface is translated; nine chapters of actual recipes follow, chunk by chunk.

Phase 2 — First full campaign vote open

The first sustained, multi-book translation effort, chosen by public vote. Candidates on the ballot:

  • On the Composition of Drugs According to Places — continue past Book 1 (eyes, ears, teeth, chest… ten books of ancient pharmacy).
  • On Antidotes — theriac, mithridatium, and the emperor's daily dose; the founding text of the antidote tradition.
  • The pulse tetralogy — sixteen books that built a thousand years of diagnosis by pulse, none in English.
  • On Crises — how a Roman doctor predicted the turning point of a disease.
  • On the Differences of Fevers — the fever theory that ruled medicine into the 19th century.

Phase 3 — The Arabic frontier later

Works lost in Greek that survive in the Arabic of Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's ninth-century Baghdad workshop — some translated by scholars already, some (like On Medical Names) still without English. These need published Arabic editions and more careful hands; they come after the pipeline is proven on Greek.

How a campaign runs

  1. The source text is split into chunks — passages anchored to Kühn page numbers.
  2. Each chunk gets a draft English translation (AI-assisted, from the Greek), checked against Kühn's facing Latin where the Greek is ambiguous.
  3. Draft, Greek, and translator's notes are published side by side, flagged as a draft until a human reviewer signs off.
  4. Anyone can challenge a rendering by opening an issue pointing at the Greek — the fastest way to improve the text.
